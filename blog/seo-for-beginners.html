SEO for Beginners: How Search Engines Actually Work

By Amit Rawat · 5 August 2026 · 8 min read

Most SEO beginner guides start with tips: "use your keyword in the first paragraph", "write longer content", "get backlinks". The tips aren't wrong — but without understanding why they matter, you're just following rules without knowing what they're actually doing.

This article starts one step earlier. We'll look at what search engines are actually trying to accomplish, and how they do it. Once you understand this, the rest of SEO starts to make sense.

What Is a Search Engine Trying to Do?

It sounds obvious, but it's worth stating: a search engine's goal is to give users the most useful, relevant, trustworthy result for their query — as quickly as possible.

That's it. Everything Google does — every algorithm update, every quality guideline — is oriented around serving the user. When you understand this, you can see why most short-term SEO manipulation eventually fails: if it doesn't serve the user, it doesn't serve Google.

Good SEO aligns your interests with Google's. You want to be found; Google wants to show the best result. Make your content genuinely the best result for a query, and you're doing SEO.

How Search Engines Work: The Three Stages

Before a search engine can rank your page, it needs to know your page exists. This happens in three stages: crawling, indexing, and ranking.

Stage 1: Crawling

Search engines use automated programs called crawlers (or "spiders" or "bots") to explore the web. Google's primary crawler is called Googlebot.

Crawlers work by following links. They start from a known list of URLs, visit each page, read its content, and then follow any links on that page to discover more pages. This is how the web gets mapped — link by link.

This is why internal linking matters. If you publish a new blog post but don't link to it from anywhere else on your site, crawlers may never find it. Every page should be reachable from at least one other page via a link.

💡 What this means for you:

Make sure your website's sitemap.xml is submitted to Google Search Console (it tells Google about all your pages). And link between your pages — a blog post should link to related posts and to relevant pages on your site.

Stage 2: Indexing

After crawling a page, Google analyses its content and decides whether to add it to its index — a giant database of web pages.

Google looks at:

  • The text content of the page
  • The page's title and headings
  • The images and their alt text
  • The page's URL structure
  • How the page is structured (semantic HTML)
  • Whether the content is duplicate or unique
  • The quality and trustworthiness of the content

Not all pages get indexed. Google may skip pages that are very thin (too little content), duplicate, low quality, blocked by robots.txt, or technically broken.

Stage 3: Ranking

When a user types a query, Google searches its index for relevant pages and ranks them in order of usefulness. This is where the real complexity lives — Google uses hundreds of signals to determine ranking order.

Key ranking factors broadly fall into three categories:

  • Relevance — does the page's content match what the user is looking for?
  • Authority — is this page (and this website) trustworthy and well-regarded?
  • Quality — is the content well-written, accurate, useful, and complete?

On-Page SEO: What You Control Directly

On-page SEO refers to things you can change on your own pages to help Google understand and rank them. These are the most actionable things for beginners.

Title Tags

The <title> element in your page's HTML is what appears as the blue headline in Google search results. It's one of the strongest signals Google uses to understand what a page is about. Keep it under 60 characters, include your target keyword naturally, and make it relevant and enticing.

Meta Descriptions

The meta description is the grey text under the title in search results. It doesn't directly affect ranking, but it affects click-through rate — which is how many people choose your result. Write it like an advert: clear, compelling, under 160 characters.

Heading Hierarchy (H1, H2, H3)

Headings help both users and search engines understand your content structure. Every page should have exactly one <h1> (the main title), then <h2> for major sections, and <h3> for sub-sections. Don't use headings for styling — use them for structure.

Image Alt Text

Every image should have an alt attribute that describes what the image shows. This helps search engines understand your images (they can't "see" them the way humans do) and is also important for accessibility for users with screen readers.

URL Structure

Use clear, descriptive URLs. /blog/seo-for-beginners is far better than /blog/?p=2847. Short, descriptive, hyphen-separated URLs help both users and search engines understand what a page is about.

Content Quality

Google's ranking algorithms have become very good at identifying genuinely useful content versus content that just uses keywords. Write for humans first. Cover a topic thoroughly. Be accurate. Be original. Answer the question the user is actually asking.

Off-Page SEO: Signals From Other Sites

Off-page SEO refers to signals that come from outside your website — primarily backlinks.

A backlink is a link from another website to yours. Google treats backlinks as votes of confidence. If a reputable, relevant website links to your content, that's a signal that your content is valuable.

The quality of backlinks matters much more than quantity. One link from a well-respected industry website is worth far more than hundreds of links from low-quality directories.

Building backlinks is one of the harder parts of SEO. The most sustainable approach: create content so useful and original that people want to link to it naturally.

Technical SEO: The Foundations

Technical SEO ensures that search engines can properly crawl, index, and render your website. Key basics:

  • HTTPS — your site should be served over HTTPS (secure). Google uses this as a ranking signal.
  • Mobile-friendly — Google primarily uses the mobile version of your site for indexing.
  • Page speed — slow pages rank lower. Google measures Core Web Vitals (loading, interactivity, visual stability).
  • Sitemap.xml — a file that lists all your pages, submitted to Google Search Console.
  • Robots.txt — a file that tells crawlers which pages to visit and which to skip.
  • Canonical URLs — tells Google which version of a page is the "official" one, preventing duplicate content issues.

How to Submit Your Site to Google

Google doesn't automatically know your website exists. To get it found:

  1. Create a free account at Google Search Console (search.google.com/search-console)
  2. Add your website and verify that you own it
  3. Submit your sitemap.xml
  4. Request indexing for important pages using the URL Inspection tool

Search Console also shows you which queries people use to find your site, which pages are being indexed, and any technical issues Google has found. It's an essential tool for anyone practising SEO.

Key Takeaways

  • Search engines want to show users the most useful, relevant, trustworthy result — align with this and you're doing SEO
  • The three stages: crawling (finding pages), indexing (storing pages), ranking (ordering results)
  • On-page SEO covers your title, meta description, headings, content, images, and URL structure
  • Off-page SEO is primarily about earning backlinks from reputable sites
  • Technical SEO ensures search engines can access, understand, and render your site
  • Submit your site to Google Search Console and your sitemap.xml to get indexed
